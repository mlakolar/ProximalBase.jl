Sparse iterates

ProximalBase provides three iterate types for coordinate descent and active-set procedures. We separate the choice of storage from the update formulas, which are derived in Coordinate descent.

IterateArray representedOne coordinate
SparseIterateA sparse vector or matrixOne entry
SymmetricSparseIterateA sparse symmetric matrixOne lower-triangular entry and its symmetric counterpart
AtomIterateA vector partitioned into groupsOne group of entries

The entries of an iterate are real. A complex element type, or a complex operand of an iterate product, raises an ArgumentError; see Precision.

Entries and coordinates

prox_entry(g, v, i, γ) computes the scalar proximal operator of entry $i$ without writing the iterate. In contrast, prox_coordinate!(x, g, k, γ) reads coordinate $k$ of the iterate, applies its proximal operator and writes the result. The calling procedure must supply the gradient step before applying this proximal operation.

The two calls index different spaces:

  • prox_entry takes an entry $i$ of the array, the index of the weights and bounds.
  • prox_coordinate! takes a coordinate $k$ of the iterate.

The two coincide for a SparseIterate. A coordinate is an entry of the lower triangle for a SymmetricSparseIterate, and a group for an AtomIterate.

SparseIterate

A SparseIterate is a vector or matrix that stores its nonzero entries in a list. Three arrays hold them: the values, their indices, and for every index the position of its value in the list (zero when it is not stored). Reading or writing any entry is therefore $O(1)$, and a product $Xx$ costs $O(n\cdot\mathrm{nnz})$. The stored entries are the active coordinates (activecoordinates, nactive). Setting an entry to zero keeps it stored, so that an active set does not change during a sweep. dropzeros! removes the zeros between sweeps. nnz(x) counts stored zeros too. nonzeros(x) gives a view of the stored values, paired with activecoordinates(x) in storage order; the view is invalidated when the stored set changes. findnz(x) returns copies of the stored indices and values in sorted order, including stored zeros.

SymmetricSparseIterate

A SymmetricSparseIterate is a sparse symmetric $p \times p$ matrix that stores its lower triangle. For indexing, length, iteration and broadcasting it is an ordinary $p \times p$ matrix, except that writing $x_{rc}$ also writes $x_{cr}$.

Read and write entries through the lower triangle, $r \ge c$, for example with for c in 1:p, r in c:p. The position x[c, r] is the same entry as x[r, c], so writing either position writes both. A loop over every position writes each off-diagonal entry twice, and the second write wins.

Whole-value writes are checked. These include the constructor, copyto!, copy!, broadcasting into $x$, map!, axpy!, axpby!, transpose!, adjoint!, permutedims! and mul!. Indexed array assignment of every position in order (x[:, :] = A, x[:] = v), and broadcasting and fill! into full, in-order views and reshapes (x[:, :] .= A, vec(x) .= v), use the same checks. They cover a view whose parent is $x$, a reshape whose parent is $x$, and a view of such a reshape. Use : or integer vectors matching each axis in order for a full two-index assignment or a full matrix view. A full single-index assignment accepts :, the integers 1:length(x) in order, or CartesianIndices(x) with its shape. copyto!(x, Q) from an orthogonal factor is checked too.

Matrix values must have the axes of $x$, with equal entries $(r, c)$ and $(c, r)$ and NaN equal to NaN. These comparisons precede conversion to the element type of $x$. Linear full writes reshape their values in column-major order for the check; x[:] = v needs $p^2$ values. If the axes differ or symmetry fails, the checked writers throw a DimensionMismatch or an ArgumentError before writing anything. Symmetric(A, :L) stores the lower triangle of a matrix $A$ that is not symmetric, and Symmetric(A, :U) its upper triangle. Each entry of the lower triangle is written once. Sources that are symmetric by their type, such as another SymmetricSparseIterate, a Symmetric or a Diagonal, skip the symmetry check. A broadcast whose arguments do not show that its value is symmetric is first evaluated at both positions of each off-diagonal entry. A function with side effects can therefore run more often and give a value that is not symmetric.

With two array operands, mul! into $x$ checks the unscaled product for exact symmetry in a buffer promoted from the destination and operand types, applies scalars and conversion at the final write, and leaves the product out when α = 0, as LinearAlgebra does. For mul! by a number, the array operand must have the axes of $x$. The seven-argument mul!(x, tA, tB, A, B, α, β), when available, uses the same checks with flags 'N', 'T' and 'C' for unchanged, transposed and adjoint operands; other flags throw before writing. fillband!(x, v, l, u) checks that v is real and writes a symmetric band (l == -u) through the lower triangle once. An empty band (l > u) returns $x$ unchanged.

Indexed array assignment, broadcasting and fill! into part of $x$, such as a row, a column, a block or a partial mask, throw an ArgumentError before writing. Logical-mask indexed assignment is rejected even for an all-true mask. copyto! with offsets or index ranges (including transpose flags), copy_transpose! and copy_adjoint! with index ranges, copyto! from a tuple, a generator or a number, and the indexed axpy! also throw before writing. Out-of-bounds indices still throw a BoundsError.

reverse!, sort!, accumulate!, kron! and copytrito! (when available) throw an ArgumentError before writing. This also rejects reverse(x) with or without dims, cumsum!, cumprod!, and allocating cumsum, cumprod and accumulate with dims. circshift! and circshift of a nonempty $x$ by a nonempty shift throw too. triu!(x, k) returns $x$ unchanged when $k \le 1-p$, and tril!(x, k) when $k \ge p-1$. Both return $x$ for every k when $p=0$. Otherwise they throw an ArgumentError before writing.

lmul!, rmul!, ldiv! and rdiv! by a matrix, a factorization, an orthogonal factor or a rotation throw an ArgumentError before writing. So does ldiv!(x, A, B) for such an operator $A$. Reductions into $x$ (sum!, prod!, maximum!, minimum!, count!, all!, any!, extrema!, findmax! and findmin! with $x$ as either destination), in-place factorizations (lu!, qr!, cholesky! and bunchkaufman!), get! and sortperm! also throw before writing. Nonempty bands of fillband! that are not symmetric, read!, readbytes! and hex2bytes! also throw before writing; these reading and decoding calls do not consume their input. Apply these operations to Matrix(x), or compute into a dense matrix and copyto! a symmetric result.

fill!, and rmul!, lmul!, rdiv! and ldiv! by a number or a UniformScaling, keep $x$ symmetric. replace and replace! visit each entry of the lower triangle once, and their count counts those entries, column by column. triu, tril and sparse return a SparseMatrixCSC. sort(x; dims) and accumulate(op, x) without dims keep their dense results. Allocating lu, qr, cholesky and bunchkaufman use dense copies.

Other functions that write an arbitrary array position by position are not checked; apply them to Matrix(x). Loops of scalar writes over every position stay unchecked. Other writes through a view or reshape, such as copyto! into a view and scalar writes through a view, stay unchecked too. Broadcasts and fills through other nested wrappers, such as vec(view(x, :, :)), are also unchecked. Two-argument copyto!(x, itr) from other non-array iterators is unchecked. A Base function can write part of $x$ before reaching a check. rand! and randn! give a symmetric matrix but draw each off-diagonal entry twice. shuffle! and shuffle give wrong values; use Matrix(x) instead.

Its coordinates are the $p(p+1)/2$ entries $(r, c)$, $r \ge c$, of the lower triangle, numbered column by column:

\[k = \texttt{tril\_index}(p, r, c) = \frac{(c - 1)(2p - c + 2)}{2} + (r - c + 1).\]

tril_cartesian inverts this by solving a quadratic equation. Its floating square root is exact for $p < 2^{24}$, far beyond any matrix that fits in memory, and it switches to an integer square root beyond that. coordinates(x) is a vector view of the coordinates.

The coordinate step on a symmetric matrix. A coordinate $(r, c)$ with $r \ne c$ moves two entries of $\Theta$ together. Take a penalty that counts both triangles, $\lambda\sum_{i,j}|\Theta_{ij}|$, as NormL1 does on the matrix. Let $G$ be the gradient of $f$ over symmetric matrices, and $a$ the curvature along the direction $E_{rc} + E_{cr}$. Along the coordinate,

\[\varphi(t) = 2G_{rc}(t - t_0) + \frac{a}{2}(t - t_0)^2 + 2\lambda|t| + \text{const}, \qquad t^+ = S_{2\lambda/a}\Big(t_0 - \frac{2G_{rc}}{a}\Big).\]

That is prox_entry(NormL1(λ), t₀ - (2/a) G[r, c], i, 2/a). On the diagonal the factors of 2 disappear and the step is $1/a$. So, as the KronQuadratic example of the D-trace loss shows, off-diagonal coordinates take the step $2/a$ where the diagonal takes $1/a$. Per-entry parameters, such as the weights of NormL1, are indexed like the dense $p \times p$ matrix and must be symmetric.

AtomIterate

An AtomIterate is a vector whose coordinates are groups of entries, partitioning $1{:}p$. Its active groups play the role of the stored entries of a SparseIterate, and atom(x, k) is a view of group $k$. Block coordinate descent for the group lasso takes, for group $k$, a gradient step of size $1/L_k$ (with $L_k = \|X_{G_k}\|_2^2$ for least squares). It then applies the group's proximal step, prox_coordinate!(x, g, k, 1/L_k), which is block soft thresholding, and which activates the group when it ends nonzero. AtomIterate(p, g) builds an iterate that shares the groups of a group penalty g.

An iterate and a group penalty whose groups are equal but were built separately also take the active-group paths. The first check compares the two layouts in full; the iterate then remembers the penalty's group array, so later checks take constant time. copy and similar keep what the iterate remembers, copyto! keeps the destination's, and an iterate built from a restricted penalty starts with nothing remembered.

Group arrays and the indices inside them must not be mutated after a penalty or an iterate is built. This also applies to arrays retained by inner constructors.

Tracking an active set

The active coordinates form a stored working set, which can contain entries or groups that have returned to zero. We keep them during a sweep so that the set being traversed does not change.

OperationMeaning
ncoordinates(x)Number of possible coordinates
nactive(x)Number of stored active coordinates
activecoordinates(x)Indices of the active coordinates
isactive(x, k)Whether coordinate k is active
activate!(x, k)Add a coordinate to the active set
dropzeros!(x)Remove stored zero entries or zero groups

Use kkt_violation to test excluded zero coordinates and restrict to construct a supported restricted problem. The Coordinate descent chapter explains the full optimality checks that must accompany these storage operations.
