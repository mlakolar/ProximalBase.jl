Sets

The indicator function $\iota_C$ of a set $C$ is $0$ on $C$ and $+\infty$ outside. Its proximal operator is the Euclidean projection $P_C(v) = \operatorname{argmin}_{z \in C}\|z - v\|$, for every step, so projected gradient descent is proximal gradient descent with an indicator. value_and_prox! returns $0$ without checking membership. Closed-form projections use exact formulas, but computed results have rounding errors that grow with the conditioning of the problem. Projections that determine a numerical rank, such as IndAffine, project onto the set of the truncated data when the data are nearly rank deficient. Iterative projections, such as IndIntersection, use a stopping rule that need not bound the distance from the returned point to the set; a returned value of $0$ is not a feasibility certificate.

Separable sets

The box, the nonnegative orthant and the ℓ∞ ball are products of intervals. Their projections clamp each coordinate, and they have scalar proximal operators for coordinate descent. A box whose bounds exclude zero does not preserve zeros (see iszeropreserving), so on a sparse iterate it visits every entry. Constrained restricts a convex separable function to a box, and the result is again separable.

IndBinary(l, u) instead allows two finite values per entry, with $l_i < u_i$. Its projection selects the nearer endpoint, independently of the step, with ties going to the lower endpoint. Alternating multiplication by a matrix and projection onto $\{-1, 1\}^n$ gives the projected power method for community detection and synchronization (Chen and Candès, 2018). Zero projects to $-1$, unlike sign(0) = 0. Bounds may be scalars or arrays, indexed linearly as for IndBox. Array bounds are stored without copying; construct a new IndBinary after changing them. Zero is preserved exactly when each pair contains zero, including mixed pairs such as $\{0, 1\}$ and $\{-1, 0\}$; then sparse iterates visit only stored entries, and restrict is supported. A proximal gradient update from zero projects $-\gamma g$, so whether it stays zero depends on the step, and no gradient-only kkt_violation is provided. With scalar bounds, Spectral(IndBinary(0, 1)) projects onto the orthogonal projection matrices.

The simplex and the ℓ1 ball

The projection onto the simplex $\Delta_r = \{x \ge 0 : \sum_i x_i = r\}$ is $x_i = \max(v_i - \tau, 0)$. Here $\tau$ is the multiplier of the equality constraint, the unique solution of

\[\sum_i \max(v_i - \tau, 0) = r .\]

The left side is continuous, piecewise linear and decreasing in $\tau$, so $\tau$ lies between two sorted values of $v$. Sorting finds it in $O(n\log n)$. Condat's algorithm (2016) finds it in $O(n)$ expected time with one pass and a scratch buffer, which the function keeps.

When $r$ is large, the level of Condat's algorithm starts far below the entries, so many entries become candidates and are then dropped one by one. When more than an eighth of the entries, and more than 1024, become candidates, Michelot's algorithm (1986) takes over: the level of all the entries, then of those above it, until that set stops changing. It is Newton's method on the left side of the equation above, from below, and each of its steps is a vectorized pass. With equality = false the set is $\sum_i x_i \le r$, and $\tau = 0$ when $v$ already satisfies the inequality.

Projecting onto the ℓ1 ball $\{\|x\|_1 \le r\}$ is trivial when $\|v\|_1 \le r$. Otherwise the projection has the signs of $v$ and solves the simplex problem for $|v|$. The ℓ∞ norms use this projection through Moreau's decomposition.

Second-order cones

IndSOC() is the epigraph of the Euclidean norm: the first entry is a variable bound on the norm of the remaining entries. With head $t$ and tail norm $s$, the projection keeps the point when $s \le t$, returns zero when $s \le -t$, and otherwise returns $(\alpha, (\alpha/s)x_{2:n})$ with $\alpha = (t+s)/2$. Arrays are read by one-based linear index, including matrices. An empty array has no head and is rejected. Only scalar steps are supported.

IndSOC(groups) projects each disjoint group onto its own cone. The first listed index is the head, even when the indices are out of order; a group of one index imposes nonnegativity. For example, IndSOC([[3, 1, 2], [5, 4]]) bounds the norm of entries 1 and 2 by entry 3, and the magnitude of entry 4 by entry 5. Groups are copied and must be nonempty. Entries in no group are free and remain unchanged; an empty collection of groups leaves all entries free. Conjugate gives the polar indicator, which requires the negative cone on each group and zero on every free entry, within the indicator's tolerance. With a single cone the polar is its negative, since the second-order cone is self-dual.

IndRotatedSOC() uses the order $(u,v,z)$ for the cone $\{(u,v,z) : \|z\|_2^2 \le 2uv,\ u,v \ge 0\}$. The orthogonal map $Q(u,v,z) = ((u+v)/\sqrt{2}, (u-v)/\sqrt{2}, z)$ takes it to the second-order cone, so its projection is $Q^\top P_K Q$. The implementation computes this projection without forming $Q$ and preserves small heads when the two heads have very different magnitudes. An array needs at least two entries. IndRotatedSOC(groups) uses the first two listed indices of each group as its heads. Each group needs at least two indices; a group of two is the nonnegative quadrant. Group ownership, free entries, linear indexing, non-finite entries and scalar steps follow IndSOC. Its conjugate is the polar indicator, with the negative cone on each group and zero on free entries. The single rotated cone is self-dual.

For the value oracle, tol = √eps(float(eltype(x))). The heads may be as small as $-\mathtt{tol}\max(1,|u|,|v|)$. In a frame divided by $2^k$, write $a=u/2^k$, $b=v/2^k$ and $s=\|z/2^k\|_2$. The quadratic inequality is checked without squaring as $\operatorname{hypot}(a-b,\sqrt{2}s) \le a+b+\mathtt{tol}\max(2^{-k},|a|,|b|)$.

The cone is the epigraph of the perspective $\|z\|_2^2/(2u) \le v$ for $u>0$, with its closed extension at $u=0$. It represents a quadratic divided by a scale, as in concomitant scale estimation, or by a selection variable shared by several coefficients. For a scalar coefficient, $\beta^2 \le as$ is IndRotatedSOC() on (a, s/2, β), stored as an array. When the selection variables are uncoupled, minimizing $\lambda_0 a + \lambda_2\beta^2/a$ over $0 \le a \le 1$ gives Berhu(2√(λ₀λ₂), √(λ₀/λ₂)). With coupled selection variables, such as a shared budget, the cone retains their joint constraint.

Exponential cones

IndExpPrimal() projects a triple in the order $(r,s,t)$ onto $K_{\exp} = \operatorname{cl}\{(r,s,t):s>0,\ s\exp(r/s)\le t\}$. The face at $s=0$ is $r\le0,\ t\ge0$. IndExpDual() projects onto $K_{\exp}^* = \operatorname{cl}\{(u,v,w):u<0,\ -u\exp(v/u)\le e w\}$, whose face at $u=0$ is $v\ge0,\ w\ge0$. Arrays use one-based linear indexing and must have exactly three entries.

Both constructors accept disjoint triples of indices, for example IndExpPrimal([[3, 1, 2], [6, 4, 5]]). Each triple uses its listed order. Groups are copied. Entries outside the groups are free and are copied by the projection; an empty collection leaves all entries free. Only scalar steps are supported. The two grouped constructors are not duals when free entries exist: Conjugate requires zero on those entries and the polar cone on each triple. The docstrings specify the tolerant membership inequalities. Projection branches use exact inequalities, separately from that tolerance.

The projection follows Section 5 and Algorithm 1 of Friberg (2023). Closed-form and heuristic candidates handle the faces and extreme root parameters. Otherwise, a finite bracket bounds damped Newton steps, followed by bisection after twenty steps. The search stops at floating-point resolution or after one hundred steps and compares the last candidate with the heuristics by distance. The paper writes the coordinates as $(t,s,r)$. The dual projection uses $P_{K^*}(x)=x+P_K(-x)$; direct reconstruction of the polar component avoids cancellation. Scaled equations and exponential products avoid intermediate overflow, including when $\exp(r/s)$ alone is unrepresentable. A component can overflow if the projection itself is unrepresentable in the output type. Non-finite entries give value Inf and project to NaN within their triple; free entries are copied unchanged.

These cones represent exponential and relative-entropy constraints in conic splitting methods. Direct proximal operators such as NegEntropy, KLDivergence, NegLog, and the GLM losses are usually cheaper when the model admits them directly.

Intersections

The projection onto a nonempty intersection $C_1 \cap \cdots \cap C_m$ of closed convex sets need not have a closed form even when each $C_k$ has one: the correlation matrices, for instance, are the positive semidefinite matrices with unit diagonal. Alternating the projections converges to a point of the intersection, which need not be the projection. Dykstra's algorithm (Boyle and Dykstra, 1986) keeps a correction $p_k$ for each set and repeats, for $k = 1, \dots, m$,

\[y \leftarrow P_{C_k}(y + p_k), \qquad p_k \leftarrow (y_{\mathrm{old}} + p_k) - y ,\]

from $y = v$ and $p_k = 0$. Throughout, $v = y + \sum_k p_k$, and the iterates converge to $P_C(v)$. The algorithm is block coordinate ascent on the dual of the projection problem, whose variables are the corrections (Tibshirani, 2017), so it converges from any corrections. IndIntersection uses this to start each call from the corrections of the previous one, which helps inside a solver, where successive inputs are close. It stops when the sum of squared step lengths in a round is at most $\max(\mathtt{tol}, 10\varepsilon)^2\max(1, \|y\|)^2$, or after maxiter rounds. Only the first non-converged call in a session warns; later calls are silent. f.converged reports only whether the step-length test was met, and iterations counts the rounds of the last call. Neither stopping condition bounds the distance to the intersection: nearly parallel sets can meet the step-length test far from it, and maxiter can also stop before feasibility. Linear convergence requires geometric assumptions, as for a nonempty intersection of affine subspaces; there is no uniform linear rate for arbitrary closed convex sets, and small angles can make convergence slow.

Sparsity

The projection onto $\{x : \#\{i : x_i \ne 0\} \le s\}$ keeps the $s$ entries of largest magnitude, a nonconvex set with an exact projection. IndBallL0 selects them with Floyd and Rivest's algorithm in $O(n)$ expected time, in place, and in $O(\mathrm{nnz})$ on a SparseIterate. It keeps exactly $s$ entries when there are ties. Projected gradient descent with this set is iterative hard thresholding. On a symmetric matrix, $s$ counts the entries of one triangle, so that an entry and its transpose are kept or dropped together. Dropping an off-diagonal pair costs $2x_{ij}^2$ in the Frobenius norm and a diagonal entry $x_{ii}^2$, so the projection compares $\sqrt{2}\,|x_{ij}|$ with $|x_{ii}|$. With diagonal = false the diagonal does not count and is kept, as for the off-diagonal sparsity of a precision matrix.

IndBallGroupL0 is the same for groups: it keeps the $s$ groups of largest Euclidean norm, and projected gradient descent with it is iterative hard thresholding of groups. With k, each group also keeps at most $k$ nonzero entries, the doubly sparse set of group IHT with sparsity within the groups. The projection ranks the groups by the norm of their $k$ largest entries and keeps those entries of the $s$ best groups.

Affine sets

IndAffine(A, b) projects onto $\{x : Ax = b\}$ with several equality constraints at once. It factors $AA^\top$ once, by Cholesky when $A$ has full row rank. Otherwise it caches a thin SVD $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ of the rank of $A$, and the projection is $v - V(V^\top v - \Sigma^{-1}U^\top b)$, defined when $b$ is in the range of $A$. A tight frame, $AA^\top = \mu I$, needs no factorization (IndAffine(A, b; μ)). Each call costs two products with $A$ and allocates nothing. With ADMM, it gives basis pursuit, $\min \|x\|_1$ subject to $Ax = b$. Constrained(f, IndAffine(A, b)) restricts a least-squares loss or a quadratic to the set, the $f$ of ADMM for equality-constrained estimators such as log-contrast regression with several subcompositions.

Polyhedra

IndPolyhedron(A, l, u) projects onto several linear equalities and inequalities at once. Optional coordinate bounds give IndPolyhedron(A, l, u, xmin, xmax). Bounds may be scalars or vectors; infinite sides are omitted. Equal finite bounds are equalities. Dense and sparse matrices are supported, and the constructor copies the data.

This projection adds linear constraints to a loss through splitting. For example, constrained lasso combines a least-squares loss and an ℓ1 penalty with equalities and inequalities on the coefficients. Ordered effects, nonnegativity with a budget, and sign constraints on compositional coefficients all have this form. For order restrictions on a partial order, a row with $1$ at $i$ and $-1$ at $j$, with upper bound zero, enforces $\theta_i \le \theta_j$. IndIsotonic provides a faster specialized projection when the order is a chain.

The dual active-set method adds violated sides and drops inequalities when their multipliers reach zero. Native equalities have free multipliers and are never dropped. A thin orthogonal factorization avoids forming $AA^\top$ and handles redundant rows. Successive calls reuse the working set; reset_cache! clears it. Calls on one function share buffers and must be sequential. The workspace uses $O(n^2)$ storage. Each search costs $O(mn)$ for dense data or $O(\operatorname{nnz}(A) + m + n)$ for sparse data, and factor updates cost $O(n|S|)$. A large working set can be expensive.

Membership uses a relative $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ tolerance, with an absolute floor in the original constraint units. Projection decisions use the tighter $8\varepsilon$ scale of normalized rows. Thus a point accepted by the value can still move under projection. The dependence threshold is $8n\varepsilon$ times the normal's norm. Rows are scaled by powers of two; scaled nonzero coefficients, finite offsets and the projection arithmetic must remain representable. Accuracy is limited by numerical rank and output precision.

An infeasibility certificate, checked to $64n\varepsilon$ relative to its normal and offset scales and verified exactly on the stored data, causes an ArgumentError identifying an empty set. This exceptional verification allocates. It preserves genuine small directions when the rank test is inconclusive, rather than declaring a feasible set empty. Numerical failure and the iteration limit (maxiter, default 100 * (sum(size(A)) + 1)) instead cause distinct errors. As for IndIntersection, value_and_prox! returns zero without a feasibility certificate. It does not replace checking the constraints of an ill-conditioned problem. Constrained(f, IndPolyhedron(...)) is not provided.

Graphs of linear maps

IndGraph(A) projects a tuple (c, d) onto $\{(x, y) : y = Ax\}$. For an $m \times n$ matrix, it factors $I + A^\top A$ when $n \le m$ and $I + AA^\top$ otherwise. Dense projections allocate nothing after construction. Sparse matrices keep a sparse factorization, but each solve allocates and may compute in Float64 before conversion to the data's floating type. The Gram matrix must be representable and retain positive definiteness after adding the identity; unsupported numerical ranges throw at construction. An operator with size, a real eltype, and products with itself and its adjoint uses conjugate gradients on the smaller system. Its rtol bounds the relative true residual of that system, and maxiter can stop before convergence; check f.converged and f.iterations. The matrix or operator is stored by reference and must stay unchanged. Construct a new function after changing it.

Graph-form splitting writes $\min_x \ell(Ax) + r(x)$ as $\min_{x,y} \ell(y) + r(x)$ subject to $y = Ax$ (Parikh and Boyd, 2014; Fougner and Boyd, 2018). ADMM then uses the proximal operators of the loss and penalty and this graph projection. The same factorization serves squared, Huber, check, hinge, logistic and Poisson losses of the predictor $X\beta$, different penalties, and every regularization parameter on a path. Row and column equilibration belongs in the solver.

Hyperplanes, halfspaces and hyperslabs

Minimizing $\|z - v\|^2$ subject to $\langle a, z\rangle = b$ gives $z = v - \mu a$ with $\mu = (\langle a, v\rangle - b)/\|a\|^2$. For the halfspace $\langle a, z\rangle \le b$, the same step applies when $v$ violates the inequality, and $z = v$ otherwise.

A scalar a stands for an array of as of the size of the argument, so IndHyperplane(1, r) is the set of arrays whose entries sum to $r$, of any size. Constrained restricts a convex separable function to a hyperplane, a halfspace or a hyperslab.

IndHyperslab(a, l, u) bounds one linear functional on both sides. Its projection keeps a point inside the slab and otherwise projects onto the violated bound, in one pass over the entries for the correction. Equal bounds give a hyperplane, one infinite bound gives a halfspace, and two infinite bounds give the whole space. Membership checks each finite bound with its own floating-point tolerance, so a distant bound does not hide a violation of the other. The normal and each finite bound use the overflow-safe scaling of IndHyperplane; see the docstring for its representability conditions.

For example, IndHyperslab(1, l, u) bounds the sum of the coefficients, and IndHyperslab(a, -c, c) bounds the magnitude of a contrast. For a linear model, a can be the difference between two groups' mean covariates to bound their gap in mean predictions. Spectral(IndHyperslab(1, l, u)) bounds the trace of a symmetric matrix.

Monotone vectors

The projection onto the nondecreasing vectors, IndIsotonic, is isotonic regression. Pool adjacent violators scans the entries, pooling each entry with the block before it while their means are out of order. Each block then takes its mean, the projection of the block onto the constants. With a stack of blocks in buffers that the function owns, it runs in O(n) time. IndIsotonic(decreasing = true) projects onto the nonincreasing vectors, and the same pooling gives the proximal operator of SLOPE.

Positive semidefinite and low-rank matrices

The projection of a symmetric $V = U\Lambda U^\top$ onto the positive semidefinite cone, in the Frobenius norm, sets its negative eigenvalues to zero. This holds because the Frobenius norm is unitarily invariant, and the nearest diagonal matrix with nonnegative entries clips the negative ones. The sets that bound the eigenvalues, such as the Fantope $\{X : 0 \preceq X \preceq I,\ \operatorname{tr}(X) = k\}$, are Spectral of the corresponding sets of vectors. IndPSD reads the upper triangle of its argument. It computes all the eigenpairs with LAPACK's divide-and-conquer syevd, or with rank = r only the $r$ largest with syevr, keeping the positive ones. It rebuilds the result from the eigenvectors it keeps with one symmetric rank-$k$ update. IndBallRank(r) keeps the $r$ largest singular values of a general matrix, which by the Eckart–Young theorem gives the nearest matrix of rank at most $r$. Both rank sets are nonconvex with exact projections. Projected gradient with them is hard-impute in matrix completion.

With partial = true, IndPSD and IndBallRank compute only the eigenpairs or singular triplets they keep; see Partial decompositions.

Orthonormal columns

IndStiefel() projects a real $p \times k$ matrix onto the matrices with orthonormal columns, for $k \le p$. If $X = U\Sigma V^\top$ is a thin SVD, the Frobenius projection is $UV^\top$. All $k$ singular-vector columns are used, including an orthonormal completion at rank-deficient inputs. The projection is unique at full column rank. Otherwise the SVD chooses a minimizer, deterministically for a fixed input, LAPACK backend and configuration. The choice need not be continuous across rank-deficient inputs. With $k = p$ the set is the orthogonal group, including reflections; with $k = 1$ it is the unit sphere written as a column matrix.

This projection gives gradient projection steps for factor rotation and k-component PCA. Projecting $A^\top B$ gives a minimizer of the orthogonal Procrustes problem $\min_{Q^\top Q = I}\|AQ - B\|_F$. The step size does not affect the projection. The alternative formula $X(X^\top X)^{-1/2}$ requires full column rank and squares the condition number when forming the Gram matrix, so this implementation uses the SVD. There is no general closed form for Constrained(f, IndStiefel()).

The value checks $\|X^\top X - I\|_F \le \sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{k}$ in the argument's floating precision. A value of zero only reports this tolerance check. Nonfinite inputs have value Inf and cannot be projected. The proximal workspace follows the output's precision, which must be Float32 or Float64; scaling precedes conversion to preserve direction at extreme magnitudes. Buffers are reused for fixed types and sizes.

Structured matrices

IndPattern(P) is the set of arrays supported on a pattern $P$, and IndBanded the set of banded matrices. Each is a product of one set per entry, $\{0\}$ off the pattern or the band and $\mathbb{R}$ on it, so the projection zeroes the entries outside, and both are separable: they preserve zeros and have scalar proximal operators for coordinate descent. IndBanded holds the size of its matrices, which gives the row and the column of an entry from its linear index, instead of a pattern. Banding a sample covariance is its projection (Bickel and Levina, 2008). With LogDetLoss in ADMM, IndPattern gives the refit on a known graph: the Gaussian maximum likelihood estimate whose precision matrix is zero off the graph (see the graphical lasso example).

The Toeplitz matrices, constant along each diagonal, form a subspace, so the projection onto them is orthogonal. On each diagonal $D_d = \{(i, j) : j - i = d\}$ it is the mean of the entries of $V$:

\[P(V)_{ij} = \frac{1}{\lvert D_{j-i}\rvert} \sum_{(k, l) \in D_{j-i}} V_{kl} .\]

With symmetric = true, IndToeplitz projects onto the symmetric Toeplitz matrices and averages the diagonals $d$ and $-d$ together. This is a structural constraint of covariance matrices of a stationary series. Covariance matrices must also be positive semidefinite, which requires a separate constraint such as IndPSD.
